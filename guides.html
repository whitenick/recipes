    Three stations, in order, left to right. The whole system breaks when you skip a station or don't dry the protein first. Breading falls off because the surface was wet — fix that, everything else follows.

    The Setup

    Station 1Seasoned flour — salt + pepper + a pinch of paprika/garlic
    Station 2Egg wash — 2 eggs + 1 tbsp water, beaten until uniform
    Station 3Breadcrumbs — panko for crunch, fresh for tenderness, seasoned for flavor

    The Technique

    • Dry the protein first. Pat with paper towels until tacky. Wet surface = steam = breading slides off.
    • One hand wet, one dry. Right hand does flour, left does egg + crumbs. Keeps fingers from caking up.
    • Press crumbs in. Don't just drop it in — press gently so the crumbs cling.
    • Rest before frying. 10 minutes on a rack lets the coating set and adhere.

    Oil Temperature

    Optimal350°F — a wooden spoon handle dipped in bubbles steadily
    Too hotBreading burns before the middle cooks. Oil faintly smoking = 400°F+
    Too coolCoating soaks up oil and turns greasy. No bubbles on the spoon = under 325°F
    FinishedGolden brown (not blond, not dark) — pull when the crust is firm
    Don't crowd the pan. Each piece drops the oil temp. Fry in batches, let the oil come back up between rounds. Greasy breading = crowded pan, not bad breading.

    Eggs are a heat-control game. Most omelettes fail because the pan was too hot. Butter should foam and sizzle gently — never brown.

    Preheat the Skillet

    • Nonstick, low heat. 3–4 minutes. Butter melts, foams, but does not brown. That's your omelette window.
    • Too hot? Butter browns instantly and eggs seize into a rubbery sheet. Pull off heat a full minute.
    • Cast iron needs medium and a good preheat — 5 minutes — before butter.

    Doneness / Method

    French omeletteLow heat, constant stirring, soft set — barely holding shape. Wet, custardy, folds on itself.
    American omeletteSlightly higher heat, let it set, then fold. Golden exterior, tighter curd.
    Creamy scrambledLow heat, move constantly, pull off while still glossy — carryover heat finishes them.
    Sunny / friedMedium, whites fully set, yolk still runny. Baste the top with butter for a set white.
    PoachedWater at 180°F (barely shimmering, never rolling). A splash of vinegar firms the white.
    The #1 rule: eggs finish off the heat. They're done 30 seconds before they look done. Pull glossy, plate soft.

    A proper sear is dry protein + screaming-hot pan + patience. Don't touch the meat until it tells you it's ready.

    Is the Pan Hot Enough?

    • Water bead test (stainless): flick a drop of water. It should bead up and skate — not sit and steam. That's the Leidenfrost effect.
    • Cast iron: preheat 5 minutes on medium-high. A drop of oil should shimmer immediately.
    • Oil choice: neutral high-smoke (grapeseed, avocado) for the sear. Butter only for basting at the end — it burns.

    The Move

    Dry itPat the protein bone-dry. Moisture = steam = gray, no crust.
    SeasonSalt just before it hits the pan. Don't salt steak 30 minutes out — it draws moisture.
    PlaceLay it away from you, so hot oil doesn't splatter your hand.
    WaitDon't move it. It releases from the pan when the crust is done — if it sticks, it's not ready.
    FlipOnce, decisively. Then baste with butter + aromatics for the last minute.
    Let it release. Forcing a stuck steak rips the crust and shreds the meat. When it's ready, it lets go on its own.

    Pull the steak 5°F below your target — it keeps cooking while it rests. No thermometer? Use the hand test.

    Temperatures

    Rare120–125°FCool red center, soft
    Medium-rare130–135°FWarm red center — the sweet spot for ribeye
    Medium140–145°FPink center, firmer
    Medium-well150–155°FSliver of pink
    Well done160°F+No pink. (Don't do this to a ribeye.)

    The Hand Test

    Relax your hand. Press the meat of your palm — that's raw. Now touch thumb to each finger, feel the same spot:

    Thumb + indexRare
    Thumb + middleMedium-rare
    Thumb + ringMedium
    Thumb + pinkyWell done
    Rest it. Always. 5–10 minutes, tented loosely. The juices redistribute — cut early and you're eating the best part on the cutting board.

    Uniform cuts cook evenly. Size isn't about looks — it's about every piece finishing at the same time.

    Standard Cuts

    JulienneMatchsticks — ⅛" × ⅛" × 2". Salads, stir-fry.
    BrunoiseTiny dice — ⅛" cubes. Finishes, sauces, garnish.
    Small dice¼" cubes. Salsas, mirepoix fine.
    Medium dice½" cubes. Soups, stews, roasting.
    Large dice¾" cubes. Braises, kebabs.
    PaysanneThin slices — ½" × ½" × ⅛". Quick, rustic.
    ChiffonadeRibbons — herbs & leafy greens stacked, rolled, sliced thin.
    Bias cut45° angle — more surface, faster cook for scallions & asparagus.

    When to Salt Veg

    • Eggplant — salt early. Draws out bitter liquid, keeps it from soaking up oil.
    • Mushrooms — salt late. Salt early releases water and you steam instead of sear.
    • Potatoes — salt the water. Season from the inside out.

    Blanch in heavily salted boiling water, then shock in an ice bath immediately to lock in color and stop the cook. Set up the ice bath before you start.

    Salt the Water Like the Sea

    A big pinch per quart — the water should taste salted. This seasons the veg from the inside, not just the surface.

    Timing

    Asparagus2–3 minBright green, still snappy
    Green beans3–4 minTender-crisp
    Broccoli2–3 minStalks tender, florets bright
    Peas60–90 secSweet, just heated through
    Spinach / leafy30–45 secJust wilted
    Ice bath is non-negotiable. Pull the veg, plunge into ice water for the same time it cooked, drain, pat dry. That's the bright color and crunch.

    Ratios are by volume. Liquid, then salt, then simmer covered — no peeking.

    Grains

    White rice2 : 1 water18 minBring to boil, cover, low, rest 5 off heat
    Brown rice2.5 : 145 minSame method, longer
    Quinoa2 : 115 minRinse first — it's bitter otherwise
    Farro3 : 130 minChewy, nutty, holds shape
    Polenta4 : 120–25 minStir often, finish with fat

    Legumes

    Dried beansSoak overnight, then 1–3 hrs simmerSalt near the end — early salt toughens skins
    ChickpeasSoak overnight, then 1.5–2 hrsTender but not mushy
    LentilsNo soak — 20–30 minBrown/black hold shape; red break down (for soups)
    Rest every grain. 5 minutes off heat, covered, before fluffing. Steam finishes the cook and separates the grains.

    Salt makes food taste like itself. Acid makes it taste alive. Salt at every step, not just the end. Acid at the finish.

    Salt

    Cooking saltFine/kosher — season as you cook, layer by layer
    Finishing saltFlaky (Maldon) — crunchy hit on top at the plate
    How much≈ 1 tsp kosher per pound of protein as a starting point

    Acid

    • Lemon — brightens, cuts richness. Finish at the plate.
    • Vinegar — sharper, more concentrated. Add a splash and taste.
    • Wine — deglaze the pan, then reduce. Cooks into the sauce.
    • When to add: acid can mute early and brighten late. Add it near the end so it stays alive.
    Salt + acid together unlock each other. A pinch of salt makes citrus taste more citrusy, and vice versa. One of the easiest upgrades in cooking.

    Every classic sauce comes from one of five mothers. Learn the base, and the small sauces are just one extra ingredient away. This is how you turn pan drippings into something worth eating.

    The Five Mothers

    BéchamelMilk + roux (butter + flour)
    VeloutéStock + roux
    EspagnoleBrown stock + roux + tomato
    TomatoTomatoes + aromatics + stock
    HollandaiseEgg yolk + butter + lemon (no roux)

    Small Sauces

    Béchamel →Mornay (cheese), Cheddar, Mustard
    Velouté →Allemande, Supreme, Bercy
    Espagnole →Bordelaise, Robert, Demi-glace
    Tomato →Marinara, Puttanesca, Bolognese
    Hollandaise →Béarnaise, Mousseline, Foyot
    The quick win: deglaze the pan with wine or stock after searing, scrape up the browned bits (fond), swirl in butter. That's a pan sauce — the mother sauces are just this, systematized.

    Smoke point = the temp where the oil starts breaking down and burning. Match the fat to the cooking method. High heat needs high smoke point.

    Smoke Points

    Avocado oil520°FBest for high-heat searing
    Grapeseed420°FNeutral, great all-rounder for searing
    Canola400°FBudget workhorse, neutral
    Vegetable400°FFine for frying
    Olive oil375°FLight sauté, dressing
    Extra-virgin olive325°FFinishing, low heat, dressing
    Butter350°FBasting & flavor — burns at sear heat
    Clarified butter450°FButter flavor, high-heat safe
    Rule of thumb: searing → high smoke point oil + finish with butter. Sauté & dressing → olive oil. Frying → neutral oil you can afford to use generously.
